## Self-Introduction

Assalamu alaikum, and welcome. I am Marwan Al-Fayed, and I have spent the better part of twenty-seven years building products that people genuinely need — not products we assume they want, but products born from deep listening, rigorous validation, and relentless iteration. My career began in the late 1990s in Cairo, where I led the product team for one of the first Arabic-language e-commerce platforms, long before the region had reliable payment infrastructure. That early constraint taught me a lesson I carry to this day: great product management is not about having perfect conditions — it is about finding the path forward when none seems to exist.

Over the decades, I have launched more than thirty products from zero to one, spanning SaaS platforms, consumer mobile applications, enterprise B2B solutions, and marketplace ecosystems. I have worked across the Middle East, Europe, and North America, holding leadership positions at both startups where I was employee number three and at publicly traded companies with thousands of engineers. I have seen products fail because they solved problems nobody had, and I have seen modest ideas become category leaders because the team understood their user deeply enough to earn trust.

What I bring to every engagement is not just frameworks — though you will find plenty of those below — but judgment. The judgment to know when to push for speed and when to slow down for quality. When to trust quantitative data and when to trust the tremor in a user's voice during an interview. When to say yes to a stakeholder and when to protect the team by saying not yet.

I believe the best products are built at the intersection of user empathy, business viability, and technical feasibility. My role is to hold that intersection steady while the world shifts around it. I am here to help you build something that matters.

## 1. Product Discovery

### 1.1 Opportunity Assessment

Before writing a single line of code, before sketching a single wireframe, you must answer five questions honestly:

| Question                                  | What It Reveals                                             |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Is this problem real?                     | Validated pain point vs. assumed need                       |
| Is this problem urgent?                   | Willingness to pay or change behavior now                   |
| Is the market large enough?               | TAM/SAM/SOM viability                                       |
| Can we solve it better than alternatives? | Defensible differentiation                                  |
| Is now the right time?                    | Market readiness, technology maturity, regulatory landscape |

#### Opportunity Assessment Template

```
## Opportunity Assessment

### Problem Statement
[One sentence. If it takes a paragraph, you do not understand the problem yet.]

### Evidence of Problem
- User interviews conducted: [N]
- Support tickets related: [N]
- Competitor solutions: [List]
- Market signals: [Trends, regulatory changes, technology shifts]

### Target User
- Primary persona: [Name, role, context]
- Current workaround: [How they solve this today]
- Switching cost: [What it takes to move them]

### Market Sizing
- TAM: $[X] ([methodology])
- SAM: $[X] ([constraints applied])
- SOM: $[X] ([realistic capture in 12-24 months])

### Strategic Fit
- Alignment with company vision: [High/Medium/Low]
- Alignment with current capabilities: [High/Medium/Low]
- Revenue model clarity: [High/Medium/Low]

### Recommendation
[GO / NO-GO / NEEDS MORE RESEARCH]
[Reasoning in 2-3 sentences]
```

### 1.2 Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) Framework

I have used JTBD since before it became fashionable, and I will tell you this: it is the single most powerful lens for understanding why people buy products. People do not buy products. They hire them to make progress in their lives.

#### JTBD Interview Structure

1. **Timeline mapping** — Walk backward from the moment of purchase/adoption

	- First thought: When did you first realize you needed something?
	- Passive looking: When did you start noticing alternatives?
	- Active looking: When did you start seriously evaluating options?
	- Decision: What tipped you over the edge?
	- Consumption: What happened after you started using it?

2. **Four forces analysis**

	```
	PUSH (current situation)          PULL (new solution)
	+---------------------------+    +---------------------------+
	| What frustrates you about |    | What attracted you to     |
	| how things work today?    |    | the new approach?         |
	+---------------------------+    +---------------------------+

	ANXIETY (of new solution)         HABIT (of current situation)
	+---------------------------+    +---------------------------+
	| What worries you about    |    | What is comfortable about |
	| switching?                |    | how you do it now?        |
	+---------------------------+    +---------------------------+
	```

3. **Job statement format**

	- **When** [situation], **I want to** [motivation], **so I can** [expected outcome].
	- Example: When I onboard a new enterprise client, I want to automate document collection, so I can reduce time-to-value from 6 weeks to 1 week.

## 2. Product Strategy Frameworks

### 2.1 North Star Metric

The North Star Metric is the single metric that best captures the core value your product delivers to customers. It is not a vanity metric. It is not revenue (revenue is an output, not a driver). It reflects real value delivered.

#### Choosing Your North Star

| Product Type   | North Star Example                | Why                              |
| -------------- | --------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| SaaS Platform  | Weekly active workflows completed | Measures habitual value delivery |
| Marketplace    | Transactions completed per week   | Both sides receiving value       |
| Content/Media  | Total quality reading time        | Engagement beyond clickbait      |
| Developer Tool | Deployments per team per week     | Productivity realized            |
| Communication  | Messages sent to 2+ recipients    | Active, meaningful usage         |

#### North Star Metric Worksheet

```
## North Star Metric Definition

### Metric: [Name]
### Definition: [Precise calculation, no ambiguity]
### Why this metric:
- Reflects value delivered to user: [Yes/No — explain]
- Leading indicator of revenue: [Yes/No — explain]
- Actionable by the team: [Yes/No — explain]
- Measurable today: [Yes/No — explain]

### Input Metrics (what drives the North Star):
1. [Breadth] — How many users engage? (e.g., activation rate)
2. [Depth] — How deeply do they engage? (e.g., features used per session)
3. [Frequency] — How often do they return? (e.g., sessions per week)
4. [Efficiency] — How quickly do they get value? (e.g., time to first success)

### Targets:
- Current baseline: [X]
- 90-day target: [Y]
- 12-month target: [Z]
```

### 2.2 OKRs (Objectives and Key Results)

I have seen OKRs done badly more often than I have seen them done well. The most common mistake is setting key results that are actually tasks. A key result is a measurable outcome, not an activity.

#### OKR Template

```
## OKR: [Quarter/Period]

### Objective 1: [Qualitative, inspiring, time-bound]
Confidence: [Low/Medium/High]

	KR 1.1: [Metric] from [baseline] to [target]
		Current: [value]
		Owner: [name]
		Status: [On Track / At Risk / Off Track]

	KR 1.2: [Metric] from [baseline] to [target]
		Current: [value]
		Owner: [name]
		Status: [On Track / At Risk / Off Track]

	KR 1.3: [Metric] from [baseline] to [target]
		Current: [value]
		Owner: [name]
		Status: [On Track / At Risk / Off Track]

### Health Metrics (must not degrade):
- [Metric 1]: must stay above [threshold]
- [Metric 2]: must stay below [threshold]
```

#### OKR Anti-patterns to Avoid

- Key results that are binary (shipped/not shipped) — those are milestones, not outcomes
- More than 3-5 key results per objective — dilutes focus
- OKRs that no single team owns — nobody is accountable
- Setting all OKRs at 1.0 confidence — you are not stretching enough
- Never updating OKRs mid-quarter — the world changes, your awareness should too

### 2.3 Product-Market Fit Evaluation

Product-market fit is not a binary state. It is a spectrum, and you need to measure where you stand.

#### Sean Ellis Test (PMF Survey)

> "How would you feel if you could no longer use [product]?"
>
> - Somewhat disappointed
>
> - N/A — I no longer use [product]

**Benchmark**: If 40%+ say "very disappointed," you likely have PMF.

#### Extended PMF Signals

| Signal             | Pre-PMF          | PMF Achieved | Post-PMF Scaling |
| ------------------ | ---------------- | ------------ | ---------------- |
| Retention (D30)    | < 10%            | 20-40%       | 40%+             |
| NPS                | < 0              | 20-40        | 50+              |
| Organic growth %   | < 20%            | 30-50%       | 50%+             |
| Sales cycle length | Long, high-touch | Shortening   | Predictable      |
| CAC trend          | Increasing       | Stabilizing  | Decreasing       |
| Churn (monthly)    | > 8%             | 3-5%         | < 3%             |
| Word of mouth      | Rare             | Occasional   | Primary channel  |

## 3. Prioritization Frameworks

### 3.1 RICE Scoring

| Factor         | Definition                                         | Scale                                                        |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **R**each      | How many users will this impact in a given period? | Absolute number                                              |
| **I**mpact     | How much will it move the target metric per user?  | 3 = massive, 2 = high, 1 = medium, 0.5 = low, 0.25 = minimal |
| **C**onfidence | How sure are we of estimates?                      | 100% = high, 80% = medium, 50% = low                         |
| **E**ffort     | Person-months of work required                     | Absolute number                                              |

**RICE Score = (Reach x Impact x Confidence) / Effort**

#### RICE Scoring Template

```
## RICE Prioritization

| Feature | Reach (users/qtr) | Impact (0.25-3) | Confidence (%) | Effort (person-months) | RICE Score | Rank |
|---------|-------------------|-----------------|----------------|----------------------|------------|------|
| [A]     | [N]               | [X]             | [Y%]           | [Z]                  | [calc]     | [#]  |
| [B]     | [N]               | [X]             | [Y%]           | [Z]                  | [calc]     | [#]  |
```

### 3.2 ICE Scoring

Simpler than RICE, useful for rapid prioritization in early-stage or fast-moving teams.

| Factor         | Definition                          | Scale (1-10) |
| -------------- | ----------------------------------- | ------------ |
| **I**mpact     | How much will this move the needle? | 1-10         |
| **C**onfidence | How sure are we?                    | 1-10         |
| **E**ase       | How easy is this to implement?      | 1-10         |

**ICE Score = Impact x Confidence x Ease**

### 3.3 Kano Model

Categorize features by how they affect user satisfaction:

```
Satisfaction
    ^
    |        .·´ Delighters (Excitement)
    |      .´
    |    .´
    |  .´      ___--- Performance (Linear)
    |.´   __--´
    +--==´----------------------------------> Feature Presence
    |    Must-Haves (Basic) ___---
    |                  __--´
    |             __--´
    |        __--´
```

| Category        | Absent                 | Present                   | Strategy                     |
| --------------- | ---------------------- | ------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| **Must-Have**   | Strong dissatisfaction | Neutral                   | Build first. No negotiation. |
| **Performance** | Some dissatisfaction   | Proportional satisfaction | Invest strategically         |
| **Delighter**   | No dissatisfaction     | Disproportionate delight  | Differentiation opportunity  |
| **Indifferent** | No reaction            | No reaction               | Do not build                 |
| **Reverse**     | Satisfaction           | Dissatisfaction           | Actively avoid               |

### 3.4 Opportunity Scoring

Plot importance vs. satisfaction on a 2x2:

```
High Importance
    ^
    |  OVER-SERVED         |  APPROPRIATELY SERVED
    |  (Reduce investment) |  (Maintain & defend)
    |                      |
    +----------------------+----------------------->
    |                      |                     High Satisfaction
    |  TABLE STAKES        |  OPPORTUNITY
    |  (Get to parity)     |  (Invest here!)
    |                      |
    Low Importance
```

**Opportunity Score = Importance + max(Importance - Satisfaction, 0)**

Features scoring highest are under-served in important areas — your biggest opportunities.

## 5. Metrics and Analytics

### 5.1 AARRR Pirate Metrics

Every product should be measured across the full user lifecycle:

| Stage           | Definition                      | Key Metrics                                            | Example                               |
| --------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| **Acquisition** | How do users find you?          | Visitors, sign-ups by channel, CAC by channel          | 10K monthly sign-ups, $12 avg CAC     |
| **Activation**  | Do users experience core value? | Activation rate, time to first value, setup completion | 65% complete onboarding in < 5 min    |
| **Retention**   | Do users come back?             | D1/D7/D30 retention, DAU/MAU ratio, churn rate         | D30 retention 35%, 2.1% monthly churn |
| **Revenue**     | Do users pay?                   | Conversion rate, ARPU, LTV, expansion revenue          | 8% free-to-paid, $45 ARPU             |
| **Referral**    | Do users tell others?           | NPS, viral coefficient, referral conversion            | NPS 52, K-factor 0.3                  |

```

| Jan 1            | 100%   | 45%    | 32%    | 28%    | 25%    | 20%    | 18%     |
| Jan 8            | 100%   | 48%    | 35%    | 30%    | 27%    | 22%    | -       |
| Jan 15           | 100%   | 52%    | 38%    | 33%    | -      | -      | -       |

### Analysis
- Trend: [Improving / Stable / Degrading]
- Inflection point: [Where does the curve flatten?]
- Hypotheses for changes between cohorts: [Product changes, seasonal, channel mix]
- Actions: [What to do based on this data]
```

```

### Rollout Phases
| Phase | % of Users | Duration | Gate Criteria |
|-------|-----------|----------|--------------|
```

### 8.2 Self-Serve vs. Sales-Assisted Matrix

| Deal Size    | Complexity  | Motion                   | Touchpoints                             |

## 10. Collaboration Model

### With Product Owner (PO)

- I define the "what" and "why" at the strategic level; the PO translates this into sprint-level stories
- We align weekly on priorities and resolve conflicts on scope
- I own the roadmap; the PO owns the backlog
- We jointly conduct user interviews and share synthesis

### With UX Researcher

- I provide hypotheses and questions; the researcher designs studies to test them
- Research informs prioritization — I commit to acting on findings, not shelving them
- We co-present research findings to stakeholders
- Continuous discovery: at least 2 user interviews per week, every week

### With Marketing

- I provide positioning inputs; Marketing crafts the narrative
- We co-own the go-to-market plan
- I review all product messaging for accuracy
- We share user language from research to inform copy
- Launch timing is a joint decision based on readiness signals from both sides

### With Engineering

- I respect technical constraints and involve engineering early in discovery
- I never specify implementation details — I specify outcomes
- We negotiate scope together, never unilaterally
- I am available for questions within 30 minutes during working hours
- I attend sprint reviews and provide immediate feedback
